**Main idea:** Manner of articulation = **how** we make a consonant sound — how airflow is blocked, narrowed, or controlled.  
Place of articulation + manner of articulation = each unique consonant sound.

**Why it matters:** If you don’t know how a sound is made, it’s hard to produce it correctly—especially as a second-language learner. Being understood is the key goal.

### The 6 manners for consonant sounds

1. **Stops / plosives**  
   Air pressure builds in the mouth, then releases in a sudden burst.  
   Examples: **/p, b, t, d, k, g/**

2. **Fricatives**  
   Air is forced through a narrow gap, creating a continuous flow/friction. Air does not stop completely.  
   Examples: **/f, v, s, z, ʃ, ʒ, h/**

3. **Affricates**  
   A combination: a stop + a fricative release. There is a sudden release, but airflow is also controlled/narrowed.  
   Examples: **/tʃ/ (church), /dʒ/ (judge)**

4. **Nasals**  
   Air flows through the nasal cavity; sound resonates in the nose.  
   Examples: **/m, n, ŋ/**

5. **Liquids**  
   Airflow is blocked through the center of the tongue but passes around the sides.  
   Examples: **/l/, /r/**

6. **Glides / slides**  
   The tongue moves/slides from a more constricted position to a more open one.  
   Examples: **/w/, /j/ (yes)**

**Note:** Liquids and glides are also called **approximants**.

### Quick takeaway
- **Stops** = burst of air
- **Fricatives** = continuous friction
- **Affricates** = stop + friction
- **Nasals** = air through nose
- **Liquids** = air around tongue sides
- **Glides** = tongue slides/moves

**Practice tip:** Learn each consonant by both **place** and **manner**; then practice the sound slowly while noticing what your tongue, lips, and airflow are doing.
